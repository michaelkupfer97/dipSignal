For years Beijing did something quieter than debating chips or AI capex cycles — **it bought oil. A lot of it.**

According to Energy Information Administration (EIA) data for **2025**, China averaged about **1.1 million barrels per day** of additions into strategic petroleum and related commercial inventories. By end-December **2025**, official and commercial inventories in China totaled about **360 million barrels** — compared with roughly **414 million barrels** sitting in U.S. strategic petroleum reserves.

The wedge had been narrowing — and Iran-related disruption made that wedge geopolitically hot in a hurry.

## Inventories as leverage

Economically what China engineered is ruthlessly straightforward. Once Hormuz tightened and Brent screamed higher, Beijing was already insulated in ways importer peers were not — able to tap stocks to supply domestic refineries and assist neighboring economies squeezed by disruptions to Persian Gulf cargoes.

Kpler analysts recently flagged that **most Middle East cargoes loaded pre-conflict already discharged**, after which inventory draw dynamics accelerate — local refining runs throttle. Whoever enters that phase with warehouses full holds **pricing power**, not only economic bargaining chips but diplomatic ones.

Oxford Institute for Energy Studies frames Chinese strategic inventories as triangular hedges — supply disruptions, commodity volatility, FX volatility simultaneously.

## The friction point: tiny “tea-kettle” refiners

Not everything about China’s oil machinery is quiet. The **“tea-kettle”** refiners — small private Chinese plants — became a direct flashpoint between Washington and Beijing by continuing Iranian crude purchases that Washington sanctions aim to block. They are small enough, and Chinese enough, to be practically difficult for U.S. enforcement to reach at scale.

China’s commerce ministry escalated by instructing domestic firms **not** to comply with U.S. sanctions targeting those refiners. Research house Teneo argues Beijing is even encouraging the small plants to keep Iranian barrels moving — an unprecedented signal that on this sanctions layer, China does not intend to concede.

Washington’s response has been muted so far; Teneo warns a **second wave** of sanctions aimed squarely at Sino-Iranian trade could follow. If that happens, Hormuz might be prologue, not the whole story.

## What Trump walks into in Beijing

The May **14–15** summit lands in a backdrop China could hardly have engineered more favorably for itself. Oil inventories reduce energy pressure Washington assumed Beijing would feel acutely. Sanctions on Iranian crude? Beijing is not only ignoring them — it has turned compliance into a controversy.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent already confirmed Iran will dominate the agenda — shoving corporate wish-list items like tariff relief, rare-earth export liberalization, and AI cooperation toward the margin.

A leaked CIA analysis — reported by U.S. media citing a senior U.S. official — estimated Tehran could sustain the U.S. blockade for perhaps **four more months**, lowering pressure on Iran while increasing pressure on Washington to close a deal before conditions worsen.

China exits this chapter with an advantage many missed: it built a buffer quietly — protection, bargaining power, and the ability to present itself as holding keys to global energy stabilization.

When Trump lands in Beijing, he is not only negotiating tariffs. He is negotiating across from a counterpart sitting on one of the world’s larger strategic stockpiles — and measuring what that leverage is worth.
