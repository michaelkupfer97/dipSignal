November **2022** gave markets a postcard moment — ChatGPT, instant consensus that the world had shifted — and Nvidia hoovered up thematic flows thereafter.

Something changed last week without anything “breaking” at Nvidia itself. The company is still projecting about **70%** revenue growth, still dominates the market for hyperscaler GPUs — and is still routinely described as the world’s largest listed company by market capitalization. Nvidia rose roughly **8%** on the week.

Meanwhile Micron jumped about **37%**, Intel and AMD each added on the order of **25%**, and Corning — a **175-year-old** glass and optics name — rose about **18%**. Jordan Klein at Mizuho called it a **changing of the guard** in AI trade, and price action looked like agreement.

## Memory shortage turns quiet companies into loud stories

Micron’s numbers almost need a pause: a market cap above **$800 billion**; a stock up **roughly 750%** over the past year. On CNBC in March, CEO Sanjay Mehrotra said major customers are getting only **“50% to two-thirds”** of requested supply because of shortages — marketing language matters less than the implication: allocation, not promotion.

Economically it is blunt. In an undersupplied market where prices spike faster than variable costs widen, operating leverage feeds straight into valuations.

## CPUs move back to the foreground

But the narrative is broader than DRAM. Investors are repricing **2026** AI infrastructure as different from **2023** hyperscaler frenzy. Agentic workloads and broader fleet orchestration restore CPU-heavy server demand alongside GPU acres.

Bank of America models the merchant market for datacenter CPUs roughly **doubling** from about **$27 billion in 2025** to **$60 billion by 2030**. AMD crushed estimates again last week — CEO Lisa Su lifted her outlook for CPU server-market growth toward **about 35% annually**, versus roughly **18%** she flagged only in November. Goldman Sachs and Bernstein upgraded the shares the same week.

## Intel optics — factories and glass older than transistor physics

The strangest subplot pairs two firms rarely cast as heroes of AI euphoria: Intel and Corning.

Intel, after losing years of process leadership narrative, reinvented sentiment in plain sight — doubling in April alone, then sprinting higher. A *Wall Street Journal* headline on tentative Apple silicon manufacturing signaled that **Intel Foundry** is migrating from investor-deck aspiration toward something markets will test in silicon.

Separately Nvidia announced optics manufacturing tied to Corning involving up to roughly **$3.2 billion** in Nvidia equity investment tied to domestic capacity, layering on earlier Corning linkage to Meta for fiber into AI campuses through roughly **2030**. Jensen Huang’s language about scaling optics “at a rhythm optical companies haven’t historically seen” is the kind of line that contextualizes optics stocks suddenly trading like growth names again.

## The warning label nobody volunteered to hear

Amid euphoria, at least one sell-side strategist waved the caution tape. Jonathan Krinsky at BTIG reiterated a comparison fatigue has not killed: Philadelphia’s semiconductor index ripping **?66% year-to-date** in both speed and amplitude mirrors **1999** — **“in some respects more extreme,”** he argued — projecting a possible **25% to 30%** drawdown scenario for the index.

That is not a crash forecast, and nobody loves dot-com comps. Still, the tempo of rotations — money jumping week to week, marginal headlines squeezing dormant tickers triple digits above recent bottoms — merits discipline.

Infrastructure booms build real scaffolding; they also rhyme with euphoria while the music still plays.
